# Severity Rubric

Finding confusion is easy; a careful reader can flag something on every line. That
isn't help — an author who gets forty flags fixes none of them, because nothing
tells them where to spend their limited editing time. This rubric ranks predicted
confusions by the only currency that matters to a busy instructor: **how much
trouble each one actually causes.** Rank well and the author fixes the three
things that were going to generate a week of email, and ignores the rest with a
clear conscience.

## The two questions that set severity

Score each predicted confusion on two axes, then combine.

**1. Reach — how many students hit it?**
The more literal you have to be to trip on it, the fewer people it catches. A word
that's ambiguous only under a strained reading catches almost no one; a missing
due date catches everyone.

- **Broad** — most of the class will hit it (a missing submission format, an
  undefined but load-bearing term, a genuine contradiction).
- **Some** — a meaningful minority (an ambiguity most will resolve correctly from
  context, but a chunk won't).
- **Few** — only an unusually literal reader trips.

**2. Consequence — how badly does it derail them when they do?**
This is the axis authors underweight. The key distinction is **blocking vs.
degrading**:

- **Blocking** — the student cannot proceed until it's resolved. They *must* ask,
  or they stop. This is the email generator. (They can't submit because they don't
  know where; they can't start because a step depends on undefined output.)
- **Corrupting** — they can proceed, but on a wrong assumption, so they do real
  work in the wrong direction. No email arrives — which is worse, because it
  surfaces at grading as a fairness dispute instead of a quick question. (They
  wrote the essay to the wrong prompt reading.)
- **Degrading** — mild friction; they guess right, or lose a minute, and move on.

## Combining into a rank

| | Blocking | Corrupting | Degrading |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Broad reach** | High | High | Medium |
| **Some reach** | High | Medium | Low |
| **Few reach** | Medium | Low | Low |

- **High** — will generate email or grading disputes. Fix before publishing;
  treat as non-negotiable.
- **Medium** — worth fixing while you're in the document. Won't sink the
  assignment, but it's friction you can cheaply remove.
- **Low** — optional polish. Name it so the author knows it exists, but don't let
  it crowd the list. It's fine to ship with Lows unaddressed.

**Contradictions are High by default**, regardless of the table — a student
penalized for following one of your own instructions is the worst case, so it
jumps the queue.

## Order by return on edit, not just severity

Severity sets the tiers; within and across tiers, let **cost of the fix** break
ties. A High that's a one-word change comes before a High that needs a
restructure — the author should knock out the cheap, high-impact fixes first. So
the practical sort is:

1. All High items, cheapest fix first.
2. All Medium items, cheapest fix first.
3. Low items, listed compactly at the end (or summarized: "four minor wording
   nits, listed below").

## Calibration examples

- *"Submit your work."* (no where, no format, no deadline nearby) → Broad +
  Blocking → **High.** Everyone needs it; no one can finish without it.
- *"Write a short reflection."* → Broad + Corrupting (some write a sentence, some
  write three pages, and the grade punishes the guessers) → **High.**
- *"Use the argument from Tuesday's lecture."* → Some + Blocking for the students
  who missed Tuesday → **High** (a chunk of the class is fully stuck).
- Prompt says "two examples," rubric awards points for "several" → contradiction →
  **High**, flagged first.
- A slightly clunky sentence whose meaning is still recoverable → Few + Degrading
  → **Low.** Mention once, move on.

## Don't inflate, and don't over-anchor

Two failure modes to avoid:

- **Inflation.** Marking marginal items High to seem thorough. It destroys the
  ranking's whole value — the author stops trusting the tiers and treats every flag
  as optional. Reserve High for genuine email-generators.
- **Over-anchoring on your own read.** You are simulating *the class*, not one
  maximally literal reader. If a confusion requires a genuinely strained,
  bad-faith interpretation that almost no real student would land on, it's Low or
  it's off the list — don't manufacture reach that isn't there.
