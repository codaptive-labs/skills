# Letter of Rec Drafter

Recommendation letters arrive in seasonal pile-ups — a dozen due the same December
week — and every one deserves the specificity and warmth that a pile-up destroys.
The generic letter, the tired letter, the one that could be about anyone: those
aren't what you'd write for a person you genuinely believe in. They're what happens
when a real endorsement meets a deadline crush and the words run out. This drafts
from what you actually know about the person, in your own letter voice, shaped to
where it's going — so the fifth letter of the night is as specific as the first.

And it won't pad. If it doesn't have the evidence for a claim, it asks you rather
than inventing it — because a recommendation goes out over your name, and a
fabricated detail in it is your reputation on the line, not the skill's.

## When to run it

On-demand — it speaks only when called. You bring it a letter to write: a student
headed to grad school, a colleague up for a role, someone applying for a
scholarship or award.

## Where it works

This works anywhere, including plain chat. Paste your notes and the posting in, or
point it at files (the CV, the job description) if your surface can read them — the
method is the same. The one thing it needs is your **letter voice profile**, which
it builds once from a past letter and reuses after, so you're not re-teaching it
your voice every season. See `references/voice-profile-intake.md`.

## The procedure

Four steps. The references carry the substance; read them before the first run.

1. **Collect the raw material.** Gather what the letter will be built from: your
   own notes and genuine memories of the person, their CV or résumé, the specific
   opportunity (the program, job, or award and what it's looking for), and any
   forms or prompts the recommender has to address. What counts as usable evidence
   — and what doesn't — is in `references/evidence-checklist.md`.
2. **Draft voice-true, shaped to the audience.** It drafts in your letter voice
   (from the profile) and in the structure that fits the destination — an academic
   letter reads differently from an industry reference or a scholarship letter. See
   `references/voice-profile-intake.md` and
   `references/letter-structure-patterns.md`.
3. **Honesty check.** Before handing the draft over, it flags any claim it couldn't
   ground in the evidence you gave it. Those gaps come back to you as **questions**
   — "Can you recall a specific time they showed the leadership you mentioned?" —
   not as smooth invented filler. `references/evidence-checklist.md`.
4. **Variants on demand.** From the same material it produces the length you
   actually need: a full letter, a short form, or a portal text-box paragraph
   capped to a word or character limit. One set of evidence, several shapes. See
   `references/letter-structure-patterns.md`.

## The rules that keep it trustworthy

- **Never invents accomplishments.** No awards, numbers, projects, titles, or
  quotes that you didn't supply. This is the load-bearing rule: a recommendation's
  entire worth is that it's true and it's yours. A plausible-sounding fabrication
  doesn't help the candidate — it exposes you, and it can sink them if it's caught.
- **Missing evidence becomes a question, not filler.** When the letter wants to
  make a claim the material doesn't support, the honest move is to ask you for the
  anecdote, not to generate a generic one. Gaps come back up; they don't get
  papered over.
- **Specific over superlative.** "The best student in a decade" is a claim that
  itself needs backing. The letter earns its force from concrete instances — the
  moment, the project, the thing they did — not from stacked adjectives. Push every
  claim toward the anecdote that proves it.
- **You own every word.** The draft is a starting point; the letter goes out under
  your name and your judgment. Read it, own each claim, cut anything you wouldn't
  say yourself before it's sent.

## A workflow worth keeping

The best raw material is the note you wrote when the moment was fresh, not the one
you strain to reconstruct in December. Keep a small running notes file per person
you might someday recommend — a line after the standout presentation, the office
hours where something clicked, the project they carried. When the request comes,
you hand this skill real memories instead of a blank page, and the letter writes
itself because you actually have something to say.
