# Letter Structure Patterns

A recommendation letter has a job, and the job changes with where it's going. A
grad-admissions committee, a hiring manager, a scholarship panel, and a 200-word
portal box are reading for different things, and a letter built for one lands
weakly in another. This reference gives the shared anatomy first, then the four
forms and how each bends it. Match the form to the destination; keep the voice
(from the voice profile) constant across all of them.

## The shared anatomy

Nearly every strong letter moves through the same beats. The forms below adjust
emphasis and length, not this underlying spine:

1. **Frame** — who you are, your relationship to the person, how long and in what
   capacity you've known them. This is what licenses everything after it; a reader
   weighs the endorsement by the credibility and closeness of the source.
2. **Thesis** — the one-sentence version of your recommendation. What is this
   person, at their core, and how strongly do you back them?
3. **Evidence** — two or three specific instances that prove the thesis. This is
   the body and the part that pile-ups destroy; it's where anecdotes live (see
   `evidence-checklist.md`). Concrete beats abstract every time.
4. **Fit** — why this person for *this* opportunity. The tailoring beat: connect
   what you've shown to what the program, role, or award is actually looking for.
5. **Endorsement close** — the calibrated final statement of support, and an
   invitation to follow up. Strength here should match the strength of your
   evidence, not exceed it.

## Academic / graduate admissions

**The reader wants:** evidence of intellectual ability, independence, and
readiness for advanced, self-directed work — and a sense of where this person
stands relative to others the writer has taught.

- Lead the frame with academic capacity: the course, the research, how the person
  compared to peers.
- Weight the evidence toward *thinking* — a question they asked that reframed a
  problem, a project they drove themselves, how they handled a hard idea or a
  setback.
- Comparison and ranking carry real weight here if you can honestly offer it ("in
  the top handful of students I've advised in fifteen years") — but only if it's
  true and you can say against what population. An uncalibrated superlative reads
  as reflex.
- Fit means fit to *this program's* focus — name it, and connect their trajectory
  to it.
- Length: typically a full page to a page and a half.

## Industry / professional reference

**The reader wants:** can this person do *this job*, and what are they like to work
with? Less ranking, more demonstrated competence and reliability.

- Frame in working terms: your role relative to theirs, the team, the duration.
- Map evidence to the competencies the role needs — not abstract brilliance but
  "shipped X under deadline," "handled the difficult client," "the person others
  went to when Y broke." Performance, not potential.
- Include work style and collaboration; hiring managers are screening for the team,
  not just the résumé.
- Fit means fit to the role's actual demands — pull them from the job description
  and address the ones you can speak to.
- Length: shorter than academic — often three to five tight paragraphs.

## Scholarship / award

**The reader wants:** merit and character measured against the award's stated
values. These letters are judged comparatively against a field of strong
candidates, so specificity and alignment win.

- Frame briefly; get to what makes this person deserving.
- Read the award's criteria and mission, and structure the evidence around them —
  if it honors service, lead with service, with instances. Alignment is the whole
  game.
- Character evidence matters as much as achievement here — who they are, not just
  what they've done.
- The close should answer "why this candidate over the others" without disparaging
  anyone — what's distinctive and hard to replicate about them.
- Length: usually one page, criteria-driven.

## Portal text-box / short form

**The reader wants:** the signal, fast. Many online systems cap the recommendation
at a paragraph or a character count, or ask three narrow questions instead of a
letter.

- No throat-clearing. Open on the thesis, not the frame — one clause of relationship
  is enough ("I taught and advised X for two years").
- Carry the **two highest-signal things** and one concrete instance. There's no room
  for three anecdotes; pick the one that proves the most.
- Respect the limit exactly — if it says 200 words or 1,500 characters, hit it;
  an answer that overruns gets truncated by the system, often mid-sentence.
- For question-based portals, answer the question asked, then stop. Don't paste a
  general letter into a specific box.
- This form is generated from the *same evidence* as the full letter — it's a
  compression, not a different letter. Draft the full version's claims first, then
  distill.

## Holding the voice constant across forms

The structure flexes; the voice doesn't. Whichever form you're in, it should still
sound like the same recommender wrote it — the profile governs tone, phrasing, and
how you calibrate praise across every length. A short form isn't a colder letter;
it's the same warmth, compressed.
