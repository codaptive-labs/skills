# Evidence Checklist

This is the honesty engine of the skill — the part that keeps a drafted letter
truthful and specific instead of fluent and hollow. A recommendation's entire
value is that it's *true* and it's *earned*: a reader trusts it exactly as far as
they trust that a real person is vouching from real knowledge. So every claim the
letter makes has to trace back to evidence the writer actually supplied, and the
gaps have to surface as questions rather than get filled with plausible invention.
Run every draft against this before it goes back to the writer.

## The core rule: every claim needs a warrant

For each substantive claim in the letter, ask: *what in the raw material supports
this?* If the answer is "nothing — it just sounds like the kind of thing you say
in a letter," the claim isn't earned and can't stay as written. A recommendation
full of warm, unbacked assertions is the generic letter the skill exists to
prevent.

## The specificity ladder

Push every claim as far down this ladder as the evidence allows. Higher rungs are
cheap and forgettable; lower rungs are what a reader remembers and believes.

1. **Adjective** — "excellent," "brilliant," "dedicated." Says nothing; every
   letter has these.
2. **Category** — "an excellent researcher," "a dedicated teacher." Better, still
   generic.
3. **Instance** — "designed and ran her own survey study when the original data
   fell through." Now it's about *this* person.
4. **Anecdote** — the small story that shows the trait in motion: what happened,
   what they did, why it stuck with you. This is what makes a reader believe the
   adjective at the top of the ladder.

The letter should be built from rungs 3 and 4. When you find a claim sitting at
rung 1 or 2, that's a signal: either there's evidence in the material to push it
down, or there's a gap to send back as a question.

## Gaps become questions, never filler

This is the move that separates an honest assistant from a dangerous one. When the
letter wants to make a claim the material doesn't support, there are two paths:

- **Wrong:** generate a plausible-sounding instance. ("She showed remarkable
  leadership organizing the department symposium.") If no symposium was mentioned,
  this is fabrication — invented fact in a document that goes out over the writer's
  name and can end a candidate's application if caught.
- **Right:** send the gap back as a question. ("You describe them as a strong
  leader — can you recall a specific time that showed? A project they organized, a
  moment they stepped up? A concrete instance will make this land.")

Collect these gap-questions and present them with the draft, marked clearly, so
the writer can fill the real anecdote or decide to drop the claim. The letter is
better with an honest hole the writer patches than with a smooth lie they didn't
notice.

## What must never be invented

Hold a hard line on fabricated specifics — these are the ones that do damage:

- **Awards, honors, titles, or positions** not in the material.
- **Numbers** — GPAs, rankings, class sizes, years, "top 5%" — unless the writer
  gave them. Invented precision is the most dangerous kind; it looks verifiable and
  isn't.
- **Projects, publications, or events** that weren't mentioned.
- **Quotes** attributed to the person or to others.
- **Comparisons and rankings** ("the best in a decade") the writer didn't actually
  make — a ranking is a factual claim about a population, not a flourish.

## The "could this be about anyone?" test

Read each sentence and ask whether it could appear, unchanged, in a letter about a
completely different person. If yes, it's filler — it carries no information about
*this* candidate and is taking up space a real detail should have. Either replace
it with something only this person earned, or cut it. A letter that passes this
test sentence by sentence is, by construction, specific.

## Calibrating superlatives to the evidence

Strong language is fine — earned. The check is that the *degree* of the endorsement
matches the *weight* of the evidence behind it. "One of the most capable students
I've taught" needs enough shown ability to carry it; drop it onto thin evidence and
the whole letter reads as inflated, which weakens even the true parts. Reserve the
strongest language for where the evidence is strongest, and let the ordinary-but-real
parts be stated plainly. A reader trusts a letter that knows the difference.
