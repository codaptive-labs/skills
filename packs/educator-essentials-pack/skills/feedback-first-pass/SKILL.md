# Feedback First Pass

Formative feedback is one of the highest-value things an instructor does and one
of the least scalable. The judgment is yours and it's fast — you can see in
seconds what this student needs to hear next. The bottleneck is the *typing*:
turning that judgment into specific, kind, criterion-anchored prose, thirty or
forty times, at eleven at night, until the comments on the last paper are a tenth
as good as the ones on the first. This drafts that prose in your voice so your
time goes to the judgment instead of the transcription.

It drafts feedback. **It does not grade — ever.** That line is deliberate and
built into how it works (see the boundary below).

## When to run it

On-demand — it speaks only when called. Reach for it when you're facing a stack of
submissions that each need written comments, or even a single draft you want to
respond to well. It won't offer itself; you bring it the work.

## Where it works

This works anywhere, including plain chat. Paste a submission in, or point it at a
folder of them if your surface can read files — the method is the same. The one
thing it needs is your **voice profile**, which it builds on the first run and
reuses forever after, so you teach it your voice once, not every session. See
`references/voice-profile-intake.md`.

## The procedure

Four steps. The references carry the substance; read them before the first run.

1. **Teach it your voice, once.** On the first run it asks for 2–3 real samples of
   feedback you've actually written and your rubric, then distills a voice profile
   it consults every time after. Samples, not adjectives — nobody can describe
   their own voice, but their past comments show it. Full intake in
   `references/voice-profile-intake.md`.
2. **Feed it the work — one or a batch.** A single submission or a whole folder.
   For each student it drafts comments tied to specific rubric criteria, so every
   piece of feedback connects to what the work was actually being measured on. See
   `references/rubric-linking-pattern.md`.
3. **It drafts; you decide.** Every draft is a starting point, not a verdict. It
   flags anywhere it wasn't confident — a claim about the work it wasn't sure of,
   a criterion the submission left genuinely ambiguous — so your attention goes
   where your judgment is actually needed. Quality bar in
   `references/feedback-quality-checklist.md`.
4. **Calibrate early.** On the first few, it offers two or three variants of a
   comment in slightly different registers and lets you pick the one that sounds
   like you. Those picks sharpen the profile, so it converges on your voice fast
   and stops offering variants once it's landed.

## The boundary: it drafts, it never grades

This is not a limitation to route around; it's the design. A grade is a judgment
only the instructor can own — it carries institutional weight, it's defensible to
a student or a dean only because a human stood behind it, and it's the one part of
this work that must not be delegated. The skill's job is to remove the *typing*
that stands between your judgment and the student, not the judging itself. So it
drafts comments and it may describe where the work meets or misses a criterion,
but it does not assign scores, letter grades, points, or a pass/fail. If asked to,
it declines and explains why, then offers to draft the feedback that supports the
grade you decide.

## Privacy, before the first batch

- **Anonymize first.** Strip student names (and any identifying detail) from the
  work before you hand over a batch — the skill will remind you if you forget.
  Feedback drafting doesn't need to know who the student is; the criteria and the
  work carry everything it needs.
- **Use an institutional account** for student work, not a personal one, so it
  stays inside the boundaries your institution already sets for education records.

## The rules that keep it trustworthy

- **Anchored, not generic.** Every comment points to something specific in *this*
  student's work and ties to a criterion. "Good job" helps no one; the draft that
  survives is the one only this submission could have earned.
- **Honest about confidence.** Where it couldn't tell whether the work met a
  criterion, it says so and hands the call to you rather than bluffing a confident
  comment. A false-confident draft is worse than a flagged uncertain one.
- **Your voice, not a house style.** The output should sound like you on a good
  day, not like a feedback textbook. If it drifts generic, feed it another sample.
- **Feedback, never a grade.** Restated because it's the load-bearing rule: no
  scores, ever.
