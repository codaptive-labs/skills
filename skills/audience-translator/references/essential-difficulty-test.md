# The Essential-Difficulty Test

Every piece of writing is difficult in two different ways at once, and adaptation
lives or dies on telling them apart.

**Essential difficulty is the content.** The concept is genuinely hard. The
distinction genuinely matters. The term genuinely has no synonym. Remove it and
the reader has not received an easier version of the thing — they have received a
different, wrong thing, usually without being told.

**Incidental difficulty is packaging.** It came from the writer's habits, the
conventions of the genre, the vocabulary of the field, or an idiom that happened
to be at hand. It costs the reader effort and delivers nothing. Remove it and
nothing is lost.

Writers cannot see the difference in their own work, because from the inside all
of it feels necessary — the jargon feels precise, the long sentence feels
carefully qualified, and the reference feels universal.

---

## The test

Take each difficult element — a term, a sentence, a distinction, a passage — and
ask, in order:

**1. If I remove this, does a claim in the piece become false?**

Yes → essential. This is the strongest signal. Simplifications very often change
truth value, and the text gives the reader no sign that they have. "Statistically significant"
flattened to "important" is now a false statement about what the study found.
"Correlated with" flattened to "causes" is the single most common example in
existence.

**2. If I replace this term with plain language, do I need more than about eight
words, every time it appears?**

Yes → essential, and keep the term. Define it once, then use it. Repeatedly
paraphrasing a technical term is worse for the reader than teaching it: the
paraphrase drifts, the reader cannot tell that three descriptions refer to one
thing, and they finish unable to use the word in the room where it will be used.
Giving a reader vocabulary is a service, not a burden.

**3. Does this distinction change what the reader would do?**

Yes → essential. No → probably collapsible. The difference between two
subtypes matters if it changes the action, and is fine to collapse into the parent
concept if it does not. This is where genuine, defensible compression comes from.

**4. Would a specialist reading this sentence learn anything from its complexity
that they would not learn from a simpler version?**

No → incidental. Much professional prose is complex as a signal of membership
rather than as a carrier of meaning. Passive voice concealing an actor, nominalized
verbs, doubled synonyms, and hedging stacked three deep are usually costume.

**5. Does this require having lived somewhere, studied something, or worked
somewhere in particular?**

Yes → incidental, and replace it. Idioms, cultural references, and examples drawn
from one specific life. The meaning is real; this particular vehicle for it is
not. Find another vehicle — never simply delete the sentence, and never swap one
idiom for another.

**6. Is this long, or is it dense?**

Long is often fine. Dense is the problem. A sentence carrying one idea across
thirty words is easier than one carrying four ideas across fifteen. Unpacking
density typically makes a piece longer, and that is the correct trade.

## When essential difficulty exceeds the audience

Sometimes the honest answer is that this audience cannot receive this content in
this format, and no amount of skill closes the gap. A rigorous account of a
statistical method does not fit in a paragraph for readers with no statistics,
and pretending otherwise produces something confidently wrong.

Say so. Then offer the real options:

- **More room.** The content survives at a longer length with a proper build-up.
- **A different format.** A conversation, a diagram, a worked example, a session
  with someone to ask.
- **A narrower claim.** Deliver a smaller true thing rather than a bigger false
  one. Usually the right answer, and usually available.
- **Layering.** The reachable version up front, the full version kept intact
  underneath for the readers who need it.

Refusing to flatten something is part of the job, not a failure to perform it.
