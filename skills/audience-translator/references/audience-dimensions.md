# Audience Dimensions

Six dials. They move independently, and the whole method rests on that. The
common failure — in human rewriting and in naive AI rewriting alike — is treating
adaptation as a single slider labeled *simpler*, then dragging everything down it
at once. That produces the two recognizable disasters: prose that is condescending
in tone while still unreadable in substance, and prose that is technically plain
and no longer true.

Read the source on all six. Read the target on all six. Move only where they
differ.

---

## 1. Expertise — what the reader already knows

The prior knowledge the writing assumes. Not intelligence, and not education level:
a distinguished chemist reading a contract is a novice, and a first-year student
who has worked retail for three years is not a novice about customers.

**Read the source by asking:** what would a reader have to already know for this
sentence to land? Every unexplained term, every step skipped as obvious, every "as
we know" is an assumption about expertise.

**Moving it down** means building the missing knowledge, not deleting the parts
that require it. Delete instead and you have not adapted the piece, you have
removed its content.

**Moving it up** is real and often forgotten: a piece written for newcomers, sent
to specialists, wastes their time with scaffolding they do not need and reads as
naive. Experts are an audience too.

## 2. Register — formality and the relationship in play

How the writing positions the writer relative to the reader. Peer to peer,
subordinate to authority, vendor to client, teacher to student, colleague to
colleague.

**Read the source by asking:** what relationship does this assume, and would the
new reader recognize it? Contractions, humor, hedging, first names, the length of
the greeting, whether disagreement is stated or implied — all register.

This dial moves *up* at least as often as down. A Slack thread becoming a proposal
is almost purely an upward register move plus a genre change. Nothing about the
expertise changes at all.

Register is also where the most damage gets done by accident. Over-formalizing
makes a warm message bureaucratic; over-casualizing makes a serious message
sound like it does not take itself seriously, which the reader will extend to the
writer.

## 3. Density — how much is packed into each unit

Information per sentence, per paragraph, per page. Distinct from expertise: a
sentence can assume nothing and still carry four separate claims that arrive at
once.

**Read the source by asking:** how many distinct things must the reader hold in
mind at the same time to get through this sentence? More than about three and it
does not matter how familiar the words are.

**Moving it down** means unpacking — one claim per sentence, the relationship
between claims made explicit rather than left to juxtaposition, a longer piece
rather than a shorter one. Lowering density usually makes writing *longer*, which
is the main reason people skip it.

Density is the dial that most often needs to move when someone says a piece is
"too technical" and the vocabulary turns out to be fine.

## 4. Cultural and idiomatic load — what shared context is assumed

Idioms, metaphors, humor, sports and military figures of speech, references to a
particular schooling, holidays, brands, regional assumptions, and the enormous
category of phrases that are perfectly clear to anyone raised inside them and
opaque outside.

**Read the source by asking:** which phrases mean something other than the sum of
their words, and which examples require having lived somewhere specific?

This is the dial that matters most for international and multilingual readers, and
it is nearly invisible to writers, because idiom does not feel like idiom from the
inside. *Ballpark figure. Circle back. Down the road. Punt on it. Move the needle.
A stretch. Bandwidth.* Each is a small stumble; a page of them is exhausting to
read, and it signals to the reader that the writing was not meant for them.

Replace the idiom, keep the meaning. Do not replace the idiom with a duller idiom.

## 5. Language proficiency — the demands of the language itself

Vocabulary frequency, sentence length, syntactic complexity, embedded clauses,
passive constructions, phrasal verbs, and the distance between a subject and its
verb.

**Read the source by asking:** how much of the effort here is spent parsing the
English rather than understanding the idea?

Distinct from every dial above. A concept can be simple, the register neutral, the
density low, and the sentence still be hard to parse because it opens with three
subordinate clauses. This dial is where the reader's effort goes into the language
instead of the content — pure incidental difficulty, essentially always safe to
reduce.

Two cautions. Reducing linguistic demand is not reducing intellectual demand, and
conflating them is exactly how adaptation for multilingual readers turns
insulting. And technical terms are usually *cheaper* than the plain-language
paraphrases that replace them: a reader who needs to know the word `endpoint`
should be given the word `endpoint`, defined once, not a rotating cast of
descriptions.

## 6. Genre — the shape of the artifact

An email, a proposal, an assignment sheet, a FAQ, a press release, a one-pager, a
lesson, a memo, a slide, a script.

**Read the source by asking:** what shape is this now, and what shape does it need
to be?

Genre is not a wrapper. Each form carries structural obligations the source may
never have had to meet, and this is where adaptation most often has to stop and
ask a question rather than produce. A conversation has no scope statement. A memo
has no pricing. Notes have no conclusion. See `genre-patterns.md`.

## Specifying a target audience

An audience is specified well enough to write against when these are answered.
Infer what you can from context; ask about at most three, and never delay
producing a draft over what you could reasonably assume and flag.

1. **Who are they, concretely?** Not "non-technical" — *the operations director who
   will approve the spend*, *first-year students in a required course*, *the
   client's engineers*.
2. **What do they already know?** Specifically about this subject, not in general.
3. **Why are they reading it?** To decide, to act, to learn, to approve, to be
   reassured, to comply. This governs structure more than anything else on the
   list.
4. **What do they do next?** The single most clarifying question, and the one most
   often unanswered in the source.
5. **What is the relationship?** Who has authority, what is being asked of whom,
   what is at stake for each side.
6. **Any constraint on the language or format?** Length limits, reading level,
   proficiency in the language, accessibility requirements, a template they expect.
