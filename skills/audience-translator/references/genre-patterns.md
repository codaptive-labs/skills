# Genre Patterns

When the genre changes, the piece acquires obligations it never had. A Slack
thread is under no duty to state a scope; a proposal that omits one is not a
proposal. This is where adaptation most often has to stop and ask, because the
missing pieces are missing *information*, not missing prose — and inventing them
is the fastest way to produce a confident, useless document.

For each target genre: what it structurally requires, and what a source in another
form almost never contains.

---

## Proposal

**Requires:** the problem stated in the client's words · scope, explicitly bounded
· what is not included · deliverables · timeline · price · what you need from them
· terms of acceptance.

**Almost never in the source:** the boundary of scope, the exclusions, the number.
A conversation about doing the work contains enthusiasm and approach; it contains
neither a limit nor a price. Ask. Never estimate on the author's behalf.

## Executive brief / memo for a decision-maker

**Requires:** the recommendation in the first two sentences · what it costs · the
risk if you do it · the risk if you do not · what you need from them, specifically
· the supporting detail below, skippable.

**Almost never in the source:** the ask. Analysis written by the person who did the
analysis characteristically ends with findings and stops. A decision-maker needs
the sentence that begins *I recommend*. If the source does not contain a
recommendation, that is a question, not an inference.

**Note the inversion.** Most source material builds to its conclusion. This genre
opens with it. That is a structural rebuild, not a rewrite.

## Assignment sheet / instructions

**Requires:** what to produce · how it will be evaluated · when it is due · what
resources are permitted · where to submit · what to do when stuck.

**Almost never in the source:** the stuck path, and the permission boundary. A
lesson plan written for the instructor assumes the instructor is in the room. The
student reads it alone.

## FAQ / help documentation

**Requires:** questions phrased the way the reader would phrase them, not the way
the organization thinks of them · one answer per question · the failure cases ·
what to do when the answer does not work.

**Almost never in the source:** the reader's vocabulary. Internal material names
things by the internal name. The reader searches for what they called it before
they knew what it was called.

## Announcement / all-staff or all-student message

**Requires:** what changed · when it takes effect · what the reader must now do
differently · who to ask · what is *not* changing.

**Almost never in the source:** the last one. Decision documents record what was
decided and are silent on everything untouched, which is precisely where the
recipient's anxiety goes.

## Public-facing explainer

**Requires:** why this matters to the reader before what it is · a concrete
instance early · the limits of the claim · no assumed vocabulary.

**Almost never in the source:** the stakes stated in the reader's terms. Expert
writing assumes the significance is self-evident, because to the author it is.

## Slide / spoken script

**Requires:** one idea per unit · the sentences sayable aloud in a breath · signposts
between sections · nothing that requires re-reading, because there is no
re-reading.

**Almost never in the source:** anything about time. Written material has no
duration. Ask how long they have; it determines what fits and there is no way to
infer it.

## Formal letter or complaint

**Requires:** the request stated plainly · the facts, dated · what was already
attempted · the remedy sought · a deadline.

**Almost never in the source:** the remedy. People describe what went wrong at
length and never say what they want done about it. It is the single most important
line in the document.

---

## Rules for genre change

- **Ask for the structural gaps; never fill them.** Missing scope, missing price,
  missing recommendation, missing deadline — these are questions back to the
  author. Prose that sounds like it contains a number and does not is worse than
  an obvious blank.
- **The order changes, not just the words.** Several of these genres invert the
  source's shape. Preserving paragraph order while changing sentences is the
  commonest way a genre change fails.
- **Cut what the new genre has no room for, and say what was cut.** A twelve-page
  analysis becoming a one-page brief loses most of itself by design. The author
  needs to know what went, so they can put back anything essential.
- **Length is a genre property.** The target form dictates length, and length
  dictates what claims can be supported. If the requested length cannot carry the
  content, that is the conversation to have, not a writing problem.
