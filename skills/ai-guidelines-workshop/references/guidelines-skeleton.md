# Guidelines Skeleton — The Structure That Survives Review

A shape for AI guidelines that holds up through stakeholder feedback and actually
gets used. The organizing move: **principles → practices → support.** Principles say
what you value; practices say what to do; support says where to get help. Guidelines
that are all rules and no support read as threat and get ignored.

## The sections

### 1. Purpose & scope
- Why these guidelines exist (the honest driver — opportunity, not just incidents).
- Who they cover and what they cover (and explicitly what they don't).
- That they're living — dated, with a review cadence, because the technology moves.

### 2. Principles (what we value)
- A short list of commitments the specifics flow from — e.g., transparency about AI
  use, human accountability for outcomes, equitable access, protecting the learning/
  the craft, privacy and data care.
- Keep it to a handful. Principles are the part people remember and the test for
  cases the practices didn't anticipate.

### 3. Practices (what to do)
- The concrete "what's allowed / expected / off-limits," built on the **augmentation
  spectrum** so it's specific rather than vague.
- Organized by the situations people actually face (using AI in their own work; AI
  with student/client/confidential data; disclosing AI use; verifying AI output).
- Each practice paired with its reason. Rules without reasons get worked around.

### 4. Roles & responsibilities
- Who decides, who's accountable, who to ask. Where discretion sits vs. where the
  line is firm.

### 5. Support (where to get help)
- Training, examples, a point of contact, a way to ask about a gray area without
  penalty. This section is what turns a policy into an on-ramp.
- A path for raising new situations, so the guidelines improve instead of ossifying.

### 6. Review & revision
- How and when the document gets revisited, and how feedback feeds in. Names the
  reality that today's guidelines won't fit next year's tools.

## Design principles

- **Design for the bewildered majority.** Not to convert the opponents or entertain
  the adopters — to give the uncertain middle a clear, low-stakes way to act well.
- **Specific beats comprehensive.** A short document that answers the ten real
  questions beats an exhaustive one nobody reads.
- **Enable, then constrain.** Lead with what people *can* do and how to do it well;
  put the hard limits where they belong, with reasons.
- **Every firm rule earns its place.** If it can't be explained or won't be enforced,
  it weakens the rules that matter. Cut it.
