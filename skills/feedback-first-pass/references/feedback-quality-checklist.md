# Feedback Quality Checklist

What separates feedback that changes what a student does next from feedback they
skim and forget. Run a draft against this before it's presented to the instructor;
a draft that fails these isn't done, no matter how nicely it's written. The
through-line: good formative feedback is *specific, actionable, anchored, honest,
and kind without being empty* — and it feeds forward.

## The bar every comment has to clear

- [ ] **Specific to this work.** It points to the actual thing — a paragraph, a
      claim, a move the student made — not a category. "Your second body paragraph
      asserts the cause but never shows the mechanism" beats "needs more analysis."
      The test: could this exact comment be pasted onto a different student's paper?
      If yes, it's too generic to keep.
- [ ] **Actionable — names a next move.** Feedback the student can *do* something
      with. Not just "this is unclear" but "this is unclear because the pronoun
      could point to either study — name which one." Diagnosis plus a handle to
      turn.
- [ ] **Anchored to a criterion.** Ties to what the work was measured on, so the
      student sees why it matters and not just that you noticed it. (See
      `rubric-linking-pattern.md`.) Feedback that floats free of the criteria reads
      as personal taste; feedback tied to them reads as fair.
- [ ] **Honest.** Names a real strength where one exists and a real weakness where
      one exists, and doesn't invent either. Empty praise the student didn't earn
      teaches them the praise means nothing when it's real.
- [ ] **Kind without being hollow.** Warm, respectful of the student as capable of
      the fix — but warmth is in the *framing*, not in fake positivity. "You're
      close — the argument's there, it just needs the evidence to catch up to it"
      is kind and true. "Great effort!" on weak work is neither.
- [ ] **Feeds forward.** Aimed at the next draft or the next assignment, not just a
      verdict on this one. Formative feedback exists to change what happens next; a
      comment that only judges the past is summative in disguise.

## The compliment-sandwich trap

A specific anti-pattern worth calling out because it's so common and so useless:
praise / criticism / praise, mechanically, on every comment. Students learn the
pattern in one assignment and hear the opening praise as throat-clearing before
the "but." It launders real feedback into a formula. Better: lead with whatever is
most useful to *this* student on *this* work — sometimes that's the strength,
sometimes it's the central problem — and let the balance be honest rather than
formulaic.

## Calibrate to the student and the stakes

Not every comment deserves equal weight, and drowning a struggling student in
thirty margin notes is its own failure — they can't act on thirty things.

- **Prioritize.** Surface the two or three highest-leverage issues, the ones that,
  fixed, move the work the most. A wall of every-error-flagged reads as
  discouragement and buries the signal.
- **Match the tone to the work.** Struggling work needs a clear path and a reason
  to keep going; strong work can take a sharper push toward excellence. The same
  flat register on both is a miss.
- **Formative, not a autopsy.** The purpose is the student's growth, not a complete
  accounting of the paper's flaws. Feedback exists to develop the writer, not to
  justify the grade.

## Where to flag uncertainty instead of bluffing

The draft should mark, not hide, the places the instructor's judgment is actually
needed:

- A claim about the work the draft wasn't sure of ("this reads as off-topic, but
  it may connect in a way I'm not certain of — your call").
- A criterion the submission left genuinely ambiguous.
- A judgment that depends on context the skill doesn't have (class norms, this
  student's history, a conversation you had).

A flagged uncertainty is a gift to the instructor — it aims their limited
attention. A confidently wrong comment they have to catch and undo is worse than
no comment. Never smooth over a real uncertainty to sound authoritative.
