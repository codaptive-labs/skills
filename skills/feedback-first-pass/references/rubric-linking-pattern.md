# Rubric-Linking Pattern

The single habit that makes drafted feedback defensible, fair, and useful: **every
comment ties back to a criterion.** This reference is the how and the why of doing
that well, so the drafts connect what you say to what the work was actually
measured on — rather than floating as unmoored reactions.

## Why link at all

Three things happen when feedback is anchored to the rubric, and none of them
happen when it isn't:

- **The student sees the logic, not just the verdict.** "This needs work" invites
  a grade dispute. "The *Analysis* criterion asks you to explain *why*, and here
  the paragraph stops at *what*" shows the student the standard and where they fell
  relative to it. Fairness you can point to.
- **Coverage becomes checkable.** If comments map to criteria, you can see at a
  glance whether every criterion got addressed — and catch the common failure of
  lavishing feedback on writing mechanics while the actual learning objective goes
  unmentioned.
- **It keeps the feedback on-target.** Anchoring to criteria is what stops feedback
  from drifting into personal taste or into whatever caught the reader's eye. The
  rubric is the agreement with the student; the feedback should honor it.

## The linking move, concretely

For each substantive comment, make the connection explicit but light — name the
criterion, then the specific observation, then the forward move:

```
[Criterion] — [what this work does relative to it] — [the next move]
```

**Example.** Rubric criterion: *Use of Evidence — sources are integrated and
support the claim.*

> **Evidence:** You bring in three strong sources, but they sit in their own
> sentences rather than backing your claim — the Rivera quote especially is stated
> and then dropped. Next draft, follow each source with a sentence on what it
> proves *for your argument.*

The student now knows which standard this is about, exactly where the work stands
against it, and what to do. That's the whole pattern.

## Working from the rubric you were given

- **Read the criteria before drafting**, and use the rubric's own language for each
  one. If the rubric says "Synthesis," call it Synthesis, not "flow" — the student
  is being graded on the word in the rubric, and matching it closes the gap between
  feedback and grade.
- **Cover the criteria that matter**, not every box mechanically. If a criterion is
  clearly met, a short confirming note is fine ("Organization: clean and easy to
  follow — no notes"). Spend the words where the work needs them.
- **Respect the rubric's weighting.** If Argument is worth 40% and Formatting 5%,
  the feedback shouldn't spend equal breath on both. Match your attention to what
  the assignment says matters.

## When there's no formal rubric

Plenty of feedback happens without a written rubric — a draft, a discussion post,
a low-stakes exercise. Anchor to the **stated learning objective or the
assignment's own goal** instead. If even that is missing, ask the instructor for
the one or two things this assignment was really trying to develop, and anchor to
those. The principle holds regardless: feedback earns trust by connecting to a
standard the student can see, not to the reader's private reaction.

## What linking is *not*

- It's **not** turning feedback into a rubric readout — "Criterion 1: 3/4.
  Criterion 2: 2/4." That's scoring, which this skill does not do, and it's not
  feedback. The link names the criterion to give the comment its *reason*, not to
  assign it a number.
- It's **not** forcing every sentence to cite a criterion. Over-labeled feedback
  reads like a form. Link the substantive comments; let the connective prose
  breathe.
