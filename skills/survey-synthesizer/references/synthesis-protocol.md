# Synthesis Protocol

The method for turning raw open-ended responses into trustworthy themes. The
governing principle: **themes from the data up, not from the question down.** The
survey's categories are how someone *asked*; the themes are what people *said*, and
they're often not the same.

## Step 1 — Read for the ground truth

- Read the full set once before clustering anything. Get a feel for range, intensity,
  and the unexpected before imposing structure.
- Note the shape of the data: how many responses, how long, how much is substantive
  vs. blank/"n/a," whether a few voices dominate.

## Step 2 — Cluster inductively

- Group responses by what they actually raise, letting categories emerge. Don't sort
  into pre-decided buckets from the question wording.
- Allow a response to touch more than one theme — real comments are messy.
- Split a theme when it's hiding two different points; merge when two are the same
  point in different words.
- Keep a "doesn't fit" pile. The outliers that resist your categories are often the
  most interesting finding.

## Step 3 — Anchor each theme in real voices

- For every theme, pull verbatim quotes chosen for **range**, not just punch — the
  clearest statement, a typical one, and an edge. Quotes are evidence the theme is
  real, not decoration.
- Preserve wording (fix nothing but obvious typos, and only if it doesn't change
  meaning). Note where you trimmed for length.

## Step 4 — Weight honestly

- Give a sense of **prevalence** without false precision. "About a third of
  respondents raised X" beats an exact percentage on qualitative data, and both beat
  "many."
- Distinguish *frequency* (how many said it) from *intensity* (how strongly) — a few
  furious comments and a quiet majority are different findings; report both.

## Step 5 — Surface dissent and minority views

- A view held by a vocal minority is a finding. Flag it with its prevalence, don't
  average it into a bland middle.
- Where the data splits, say so — "responses divided sharply on X" is more honest and
  more useful than a manufactured consensus.

## Step 6 — Read the quality of the data

- **Sample and self-selection.** Who responded, who didn't, and how that skews things
  (the delighted and the furious answer; the middle often doesn't).
- **Non-response.** How many skipped or gave empty answers, and what that might mean.
- **Limits.** State plainly what this data cannot tell you. Qualitative synthesis
  describes what respondents raised; it doesn't measure the population.

## Guardrails

- **Don't invent themes to fill a report.** If a theme is thin, say it's thin.
- **Don't launder your prior into "the data."** The discipline of quotes-per-theme is
  what keeps the synthesis honest — if you can't quote it, you can't claim it.
