# Confusion Taxonomy

The categories of where students actually get lost. Use these to structure the
cold read: for each one, ask the diagnostic question, then scan the material for
the sub-types. Naming the category matters — it tells the author *what kind* of
fix a spot needs, and a named pattern is easier to trust than a vague "this felt
off."

Four families, roughly in order of how often they bite:

1. **Ambiguity** — the words allow more than one reading.
2. **Hidden prerequisites** — the material assumes something the student doesn't have.
3. **Sequencing** — the order fights the student's need.
4. **Cognitive load** — it's clear, but it's too much to hold at once.

And one that outranks all four when it appears: **contradiction** (see the note at
the end). Two parts of the material that disagree generate email with near
certainty, so surface those first.

---

## 1. Ambiguity

**Diagnostic question:** *Can a capable reader take this sentence two ways that
lead to different work?* If yes, the literal student will pick one — often the one
you didn't mean — and act on it.

Sub-types to scan for:

- **Referential ambiguity.** "It," "this," "the document," "the above" — when more
  than one thing could be the referent. *"Attach it to your submission"* — attach
  what?
- **Scope ambiguity.** An instruction that doesn't bound itself. *"Cite your
  sources."* How many? Which style? Does one count?
- **Quantifier vagueness.** "Briefly," "in detail," "a few," "as needed,"
  "thorough." These feel specific to the author, who has a number in mind, and
  mean nothing reproducible to the reader. *"Write a short response"* — a sentence?
  a page?
- **Undefined or overloaded terms.** A word used in a course-specific or technical
  sense with no definition, or a word whose everyday meaning fights its academic
  one. *"Make an argument"* reads as "have a disagreement" to a student who hasn't
  met the rhetorical sense.
- **Optional-or-required drift.** "You might want to," "consider," "feel free to" —
  the author means one of *encouraged*, *required*, or *truly optional*, and the
  student can't tell which. Grades have turned on this.

**Before:** "Respond to the reading and use evidence."
**After:** "Write a 300–400 word response that makes one claim about the reading
and supports it with at least two direct quotations from the text."

## 2. Hidden prerequisites

**Diagnostic question:** *What does the student need to already know, have, or be
able to do for this instruction to make sense — and did the material give it to
them?* The prerequisite is invisible to the author precisely because they have it.

Sub-types to scan for:

- **Assumed prior knowledge.** A concept, method, or result from earlier in the
  term (or from a prerequisite course) treated as obvious. The student who was
  sick that week, or never fully got it, is now stuck and doesn't know why.
- **Assumed access or setup.** Assumes they have the software installed, the
  account created, the file downloaded, the reading in hand, the tool licensed.
  *"Open the dataset in the analysis tool"* — which tool, and do they have it?
- **Assumed conventions.** Submission format, file naming, citation style, where to
  turn it in, whether late work is accepted — norms the author holds and never
  stated. Often the single largest source of "wait, how do I…" email.
- **Assumed vocabulary.** Jargon and acronyms expanded nowhere. The first use of a
  term of art is a prerequisite the material can supply in a clause.

**Before:** "Submit a properly formatted APA paper."
**After:** "Submit your paper in APA 7th-edition format (title page, double-spaced,
references list). If you're new to APA, use the template linked in Module 1."

## 3. Sequencing

**Diagnostic question:** *Does the material give the student each thing at the
moment they need it — or after?* Right information in the wrong order reads as
missing information.

Sub-types to scan for:

- **Forward references.** An instruction depends on something the material explains
  later. The student hits step 2, needs a definition that arrives in step 5, and
  stalls.
- **Buried constraints.** The load-bearing limit — page count, word cap, the one
  banned approach, the required tool — appears *after* the how-to, so the student
  plans against a spec they haven't read yet and has to redo the plan.
- **Scattered logistics.** Due date in one place, submission method in another,
  late policy in a third, file format in a fourth. Each is stated; none is findable
  when needed. Consolidate logistics into one block.
- **Implicit step dependencies.** Step B silently assumes the output of step A
  ("using your results from above") without the student having been told to keep
  that output, or in what form.

**Before:** [three paragraphs of how to research and draft] … "Note: essays over
2 pages will not be read."
**After:** Lead with the spec — "Write a 2-page essay (hard limit)." — *then* the
how-to, so every downstream instruction is planned against the real constraint.

## 4. Cognitive load

**Diagnostic question:** *Is the instruction clear but asking the student to hold
too much in their head at once?* Here nothing is ambiguous or missing; the
material simply exceeds working memory, and the student drops a requirement not
from carelessness but from overload.

Sub-types to scan for:

- **Instruction density.** Many distinct requirements strung into one paragraph
  with "and also… and be sure to… and don't forget." Each is fine; together they
  can't be tracked. A checklist or numbered list offloads the tracking onto the
  page.
- **Nested conditionals.** "If you chose option A, then unless you're in the
  honors section, do X, but if B, do Y." Branching logic that the student has to
  simulate. Split by path: "Option A students: …  Option B students: …".
- **Long chains before a payoff.** Multi-step procedures where the student can't
  tell if they're on track until the very end. Insert a checkpoint — "you should
  now have a working query" — so they can self-correct midway.
- **Format working against the content.** A dense wall of prose for material that
  is really a list, a table, or a sequence. The right shape carries half the load.

**Before:** "Your project should analyze a dataset and also include a literature
review with at least five peer-reviewed sources and a methods section and a
discussion of limitations and be submitted as a PDF with your name in the
filename."
**After:** A five-item checklist — dataset analysis, literature review (≥5
peer-reviewed sources), methods, limitations, submission format — that the student
can tick off.

## The one that outranks the rest: contradiction

When two parts of the material disagree, that is not a mild confusion — it is a
guaranteed email, and often a fairness complaint, because the student who followed
one instruction was penalized by the other. Scan specifically for these across the
whole document, not just within a paragraph:

- The **prompt vs. the rubric** (prompt asks for three sources; rubric grades
  five).
- The **word count vs. the scope** (500 words to "fully analyze" something that
  needs 2,000).
- The **due date** stated two different ways.
- **The example vs. the instructions** (the sample the author provided breaks a
  rule the instructions set).

Any contradiction you find is High severity by default. Flag it first, and quote
both conflicting passages so the author can see the collision and choose which one
is right.
