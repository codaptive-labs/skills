# Building the roster

The roster determines the quality of everything downstream. Miss a stakeholder and
the simulation is silent on the objection that actually kills the proposal. Pad the
roster with people who do not care and the synthesis fills with noise you have to
sort through.

Work the six positions below. Someone can occupy two; that is worth noting, because
a person who is both implementer and gatekeeper matters more to a rollout than
anyone else on the list.

## The six positions

**1. The decider.** Whoever can formally approve or kill this. Sometimes an
individual, often a body — a committee, a cabinet, a board. If it is a body,
simulate the body's dominant interest, and simulate any individual member you know
holds a different one separately.

**2. The gatekeepers.** Cannot approve it, can stop it. Legal, IT security,
procurement, compliance, accessibility, privacy, risk, HR, a union, an IRB, an
accreditor. Gatekeepers are the most reliably underestimated group, because their
objection usually arrives late, is framed as procedural rather than substantive,
and is nearly impossible to argue with once stated. Include every gatekeeper whose
domain the proposal touches even glancingly.

**3. The implementers.** The people who inherit the work when this is approved.
They are rarely in the approval conversation and they determine whether the thing
actually happens. An implementer who was not consulted will comply slowly, which is
indistinguishable from failure at six months.

**4. The affected.** They bear the consequence and frequently have no vote —
students, frontline staff, customers, patients, contingent employees. Their
reaction still matters: it is what the decider will be asked about, and it is where
the proposal's reputation is made. Simulate them even when they have no formal
standing.

**5. The adjacent claimants.** Whoever believes this is their territory. Another
department that has been building something similar, a role whose remit this
overlaps, a group that ran a failed version of this two years ago. The objection
here is almost never on the merits and almost always about jurisdiction, which
means arguing the merits does not resolve it.

**6. The absent.** Anyone the proposal assumes will cooperate but who has
not been asked. A vendor, a partner institution, a shared services team, a
downstream unit whose calendar this depends on. Find them by reading the proposal
for every verb whose subject is not you.

## The stall test

After the six positions, ask one more question: **who can stall this indefinitely
without ever saying no?** That is a different question from who can block it, and
it catches the figure the other categories miss — the person whose sign-off is
required, whose queue is long, and who has no incentive to prioritize you. Add them
to the roster even if they have no opinion on the substance at all. Their reaction
is about load, not merit, and it is a real reaction.

## Reading the position onto your sector

The six positions are stable; the job titles are not. Translate before you build
personas.

| Decider | Dean, provost, senate, cabinet | VP, exec team, steering committee | Executive director, board, agency head |
| Gatekeepers | IT security, general counsel, IRB, accessibility, registrar, accreditor | Legal, InfoSec, procurement, compliance, finance | Legal, audit, grants compliance, records |

## Sizing

Six to eight personas is a complete simulation. Below four, positions are being
collapsed that behave differently. Above ten, the synthesis stops being readable and
the marginal persona is usually a near-duplicate of one already on the roster.

When more than ten candidates surface, cut by **exposure × influence**: how directly
does this change their world, and how much say do they have over whether it
proceeds. Keep anyone high on either axis. Drop anyone low on both, and say in the
roster that you dropped them, so the user can put one back.

## Present it before simulating

Show the roster as a short list — position, who fills it here, and one line on why
they are exposed — and stop. The user knows things the mapping cannot: which
committee chair actually decides, that Legal has been burned by something adjacent,
that the group you listed as affected has no functioning voice. This correction
costs one exchange and improves every persona downstream.
