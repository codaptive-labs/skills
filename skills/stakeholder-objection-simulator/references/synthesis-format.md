# Output shapes

Two formats: what each isolated persona returns, and what the user finally reads.

## The sealed reaction (what each subagent returns)

Every persona subagent returns exactly this, and nothing else. Keep it tight — six
to eight of these get synthesized, and verbose reactions make the synthesis
unreadable.

```
STAKEHOLDER: <position — role>

FIRST REACTION
<2–3 sentences, in their voice, on reading this the way they actually encounter it>

WHAT THEY SAY OUT LOUD
<the objection as it would be voiced in the meeting or written in a reply —
one or two, phrased the way this person would actually phrase it>

WHAT IS ACTUALLY DRIVING IT
<the interest underneath: the metric, the exposure, the precedent, the history.
If the stated objection IS the real reason, say so — sometimes it is.>

VERDICT: <blocks | resists | tolerates | supports | supports with conditions>

WHAT WOULD MOVE THEM
<the specific change, consultation, guarantee, or piece of evidence that
converts this reaction. "Nothing" is a legitimate answer, and an important one.>

RESTS ON
<which profile fields this reaction depends on, with their KNOWN/INFERRED tags>
```

Rules for the persona writing it:

- Steelman. Give the strongest honest version of this position.
- React to what is in front of you, not to what a person in this role generically
  cares about. If the proposal genuinely does not affect you, say that — a
  stakeholder who shrugs is real information.
- Do not soften. You have no relationship with the author and no reason to be kind.
- Do not perform hostility either. Most resistance is polite, procedural, and slow.

## The synthesis (what the user reads)

Assembled only after every reaction is back. Two parts.

### Part 1 — The cards

One compact card per stakeholder, ordered by verdict severity (blocks first,
supports last):

```
<POSITION — ROLE>            VERDICT: <blocks | resists | tolerates | supports>
Says:     <the voiced objection, one line>
Means:    <the interest underneath, one line>
Moves if: <what would change it, one line>          [KNOWN | INFERRED]
```

### Part 2 — The cross-cutting read

This is the part with the value in it. One screen, five sections, and skip any
section that is genuinely empty rather than padding it:

**Must be answered before this moves.** The objections that will stop the proposal
if they are not addressed in the document itself. Usually two or three. Rank them.

**Genuine conflicts.** Where two stakeholders want incompatible things and no
version of the proposal satisfies both. Name both sides and what each would have to
give up. Do not resolve it — this is a decision the user has to make knowingly, and
the most common failure in stakeholder work is discovering the conflict in the room
instead of before it.

**Cheap fixes.** Changes that neutralize several objections at once relative to what
they cost — a consultation held before rather than after, a sentence naming who owns
support, a pilot scope, an explicit sunset date. These are the highest-return edits
and they are usually small.

**Your allies.** Who supports this, what argument reaches them, and what they can
carry that you cannot. A proposal with a named advocate inside the room behaves
differently from one without.

**Verify before relying on this.** Every finding that rests only on INFERRED profile
fields, phrased as a question to go ask a real person. This section is what keeps
the simulation honest: it marks the boundary between what was reasoned and what was
known.

## Cap and close

Part 2 stays on one screen. If it is running long, the cross-cutting read is being
padded with restatements of the cards.

Close with a single line making clear this is how the room reacts, not a verdict on
whether the proposal is right — and that proceeding straight through an objection is
a legitimate choice, now made with the objection in view.
