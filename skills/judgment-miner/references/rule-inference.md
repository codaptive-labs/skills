# Rule inference — reading the boundary the rulings trace

Runs once, on a closed set of rulings, after the session cap is reached. Nothing here is
said out loud while rulings are still being collected.

## Stating the rule

Four parts, in this order:

1. **The rule in plain language**, in words the user did not say. Restating their own
   phrasing back to them is not a finding. "You discount on institutional type, not on
   budget — a well-funded public district gets the rate a struggling for-profit does not"
   is a finding.
2. **The axes it depends on**, and — just as useful — the axes it turned out not to depend
   on. "Lead time did not move a single ruling" saves the user an argument they have
   probably been having with themselves.
3. **The thresholds, as intervals**, each with the two rulings that fix it.
4. **What is still open** — axes unprobed, and boundaries the session located only coarsely.

Then the payoff finding, if there is one: the case where they contradicted themselves, or
the variable that turned out to be doing the work.

## Contradiction or hidden variable

Two rulings conflict when the same values on every mapped axis produced different answers.
There are two readings, and the order in which you test them is not optional.

**Test the hidden-variable reading first.** An unnamed factor doing real work is both the
more common explanation and the more valuable finding. The user is usually not being
irrational; they are tracking something the map does not have a column for.

The test is a probe, not an argument:

1. List what actually differed between the two conflicting cases, including surface detail
   the map ignores — who was involved, when it happened, what else was going on.
2. Pick the most plausible unmapped difference. That is the candidate axis.
3. Build a fresh pair of cases identical on every mapped axis and differing only on the
   candidate.
4. Present it. If the ruling splits along the candidate, the axis is real: add it to the map,
   and the contradiction dissolves into a more precise rule.
5. If the ruling does not split, try the next candidate. After two failed candidates, the
   inconsistency reading is the better one.

**Only then report inconsistency**, and report it as an observation with the two rulings
attached, not as a verdict on the user's judgment. Say which ruling they would keep if they
had to keep one, and let them answer. Frequently they will revise one on the spot — that is
the skill working, and the revision goes in the file with its date.

Where a conflict came from the blind re-ask specifically, note that the two cases were the
same case. That is a stronger claim than a conflict between two different cases, and it
should be labeled as such.

## What not to do with the rulings

- **Do not average them.** A rule is a boundary, not a central tendency. Eleven yeses and
  three noes is not "usually yes"; the three noes are where the information is.
- **Do not smooth the awkward ones out.** A ruling that fits nothing is either a hidden
  variable or a genuine exception, and both belong in the file.
- **Do not extrapolate past the probed range.** If nothing above 300 people was ever ruled
  on, the rule says nothing about 3,000. State the range the rule is valid over.
- **Do not convert the rule into policy language** unless asked. Extracted rules describe
  what the user has been doing. Policy is a decision about what they will do, and collapsing
  the two robs them of the chance to look at their own rule and decide they dislike it — which
  is a real and useful outcome of this skill.

## Confidence

Mark each rule with what supports it, in plain terms: how many rulings, whether the threshold
was narrowed or only bracketed, and whether the re-ask held. A rule resting on three rulings
and a rule resting on fourteen should not read the same way in the file, because apply mode
will act on both.
