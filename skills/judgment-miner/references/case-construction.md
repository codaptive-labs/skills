# Case construction — building probes that mean something

A ruling is data only if you know what it was a ruling *about*. That is the whole
discipline here: construct cases so that a flip in the answer can be attributed to one
thing.

## The shape of a probe

**A real anchor, with exactly one variable changed.**

> Last March you gave the Riverside district 20% off a half-day workshop. Same ask, same
> fee, but it is a for-profit bootcamp. Same answer?

Three properties make that work:

1. **Recognizable.** It happened. The user can rule in seconds without constructing a
   fictional world first.
2. **One thing moved.** Org type changed; fee, format, and timing held. A flip is
   attributable.
3. **Ruleable as stated.** It asks for a decision, not an essay.

Present one case at a time. Ask for the ruling and, optionally, a few words of reason — but
never require the reason. The reason is often a rationalization; the ruling is the evidence.

## Bad probes, and why

| Probe | What is wrong |
|---|---|
| "Imagine a client asks for a discount." | No anchor. Nothing to recognize, nothing held constant. |
| "A small for-profit startup with a tight deadline wants 30% off remote delivery." | Four variables moved at once from the anchor. A flip tells you nothing. |
| "Would you say your pricing is values-driven or market-driven?" | A question about self-image, not a case. |
| "Riverside asked for 20% and you said yes. Was that right?" | Invites revision of a settled ruling instead of probing a new one. |
| "A district asks for 20% off. Also, how do you generally think about nonprofits?" | Two asks in one. The second one leaks the pattern. |

## The boundary search

Not a questionnaire. A search for where the answer changes.

1. **Start from a confident anchor.** A case ruled instantly, with no hesitation. If the
   first case is already hard, back off to an easier one — you need a known side of the line
   before you can find the line.
2. **Step along one axis** in the direction of the harder answer. Coarse steps first.
3. **Watch for the flip.** When the ruling changes, the boundary is between the last two
   cases.
4. **Narrow.** Two or three cases inside that interval, halving each time.
5. **State the threshold as an interval**, never a false point value: *"between a 40-person
   nonprofit and a 200-person nonprofit"* is honest; *"at 120 people"* is invented.
6. **Then test the interaction.** Re-run the flip case with a second axis moved, to see
   whether the threshold itself shifts. Interactions are where the interesting rules live —
   a threshold that moves when travel is involved is a different rule than a threshold that
   does not.

Budget roughly 12–18 rulings for one axis pair including interaction tests. That is most of
a session, which is why v1 mines one domain at a time.

## Handling the answers that are not rulings

- **"It depends on X"** — the best possible answer. X is an axis you had not mapped. Add it,
  and re-ask the current case with X pinned to a specific value.
- **"I'd need more information"** — ask what information, then supply it and re-ask. What
  they asked for is an axis.
- **"Both are fine"** — the case is inside a region where the axis does not matter. Note
  the insensitive range; it is a real finding and it bounds the rule.
- **A long hedge** — usually means the case sits on the boundary. Record it as boundary-
  adjacent and probe just outside it in both directions.

## The blind re-ask

Once per session, re-present a case already ruled, re-dressed: different names, different
surface detail, same values on every axis that matters. Place it at least four cases away
from the original and never flag it.

If the two rulings disagree, you have measured an order effect or a genuine inconsistency
rather than guessed at one. Report it as a number — "one of your eleven rulings reversed on
re-ask" — and treat it in the analysis exactly like any other conflict, through the
hidden-variable test first.

A session with no re-ask has no internal-consistency check at all, so do not skip it.

## Order

Vary the direction of travel. Walking every axis from easy-yes toward hard-no trains a
rhythm of agreement and then reluctant refusal, and the flip point drifts with it. Alternate
which end you start from, and interleave axes rather than exhausting one before starting the
next.
