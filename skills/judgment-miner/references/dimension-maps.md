# Dimension maps — the axes a domain varies along

An axis is a property of a case that could plausibly move the answer. The map is the set of
them. Get the map wrong and the probing wanders; get it right and twelve rulings locate a
real rule.

Four starter maps follow, for the domains this comes up in most. They exist for the cold
start — enough to generate a credible first probe when there is little context to derive
from. Treat them as a starting hypothesis, not a fixed list: drop axes the user's precedents
show are irrelevant, and add the ones they mention unprompted.

## Deriving a map for a domain not listed

1. Collect every precedent you can find in the domain, with its outcome.
2. Sort them by outcome. Two piles: the yeses and the nos (or the tiers, if it is not binary).
3. **Ask what differs between the piles.** Every property that differs is a candidate axis.
4. **Then ask what differs *within* a pile.** A property that varies freely inside one
   outcome is probably not driving anything — good to know before spending rulings on it.
5. Present the candidate list to the user, ask what is missing, and start with the two axes
   they hesitate over. Hesitation marks the boundary.

Aim for four to seven axes. More than that and you cannot cover any of them; fewer and you
are probably describing something coarser than the real rule.

## Pricing, fees, and discounts

**Axes:** organization type (public / nonprofit / for-profit / individual) · budget size ·
audience size · format and delivery burden (remote, local, travel) · reusability of the
material afterward · lead time · referral source and relationship · strategic value beyond
the fee.

**Anchors to look for:** past proposals, invoices, engagement notes, any record of a rate
quoted and a rate accepted.

**Interactions that usually matter:** travel against fee (the threshold moves), and referral
source against everything (a warm referral often suspends the other rules entirely — worth
confirming rather than assuming).

## Hiring and candidate calls

**Axes:** years of relevant experience · credential match versus demonstrated work ·
seniority of the role · salary band position · remote or on-site · referral or cold ·
trajectory (rising, plateaued, returning) · specific gap the hire fills.

**Anchors:** past hires and the ones passed on, interview notes, scorecards.

**Handle with care.** Anonymize candidates in the rules file. This domain also has legal
exposure that pricing does not: an extracted rule can turn out to be a proxy for a protected
characteristic. If an axis a user is ruling on correlates with age, national origin,
parental status, or disability, name it plainly when you report the rule and recommend
review before it becomes anything official. Do not editorialize; do state it.

## Approvals and exceptions

Spend approvals, deadline extensions, policy waivers, access requests — anywhere someone
asks you to say yes to something outside the normal path.

**Axes:** size of the ask · who is asking (seniority, track record, relationship) · reason
given · precedent risk (does saying yes create an expectation) · reversibility · who else
finds out · whether the rule being bent is yours or inherited · time pressure.

**Anchors:** past exception requests and their outcomes; email or ticket history.

**Interactions that usually matter:** precedent risk against size — many people will approve
a large one-off and refuse a small ask that sets an expectation. That inversion is a
frequent, satisfying finding, and it is invisible until you probe for it.

## AI-use rulings

What is allowed on a piece of work — an assignment, a report, a publication, a client
deliverable.

**Axes:** which stage of the work (ideation, drafting, editing, verification, translation) ·
what the work is meant to demonstrate · whether the output is attributed to a person ·
disclosure present or absent · stakes of an error · whether a human verified the result ·
who the audience is.

**Anchors:** past rulings on specific submissions, existing syllabus or team language,
decisions made in conversation.

**Interactions that usually matter:** stage against what the work demonstrates. Most people
hold that AI at the ideation stage is fine unless ideation is the thing being assessed —
which means the real rule is about the demonstrated skill, not about the tool, and that is
worth surfacing in those words.

language from the **Assessment AI Policy Writer** rather than inventing a parallel set of
terms.
