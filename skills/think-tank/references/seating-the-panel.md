# Seating the panel

Three decisions before anything runs: which analysts sit, what context they all receive, and
which of them get outside research.

## Step 1 — Assemble the context every analyst receives

Every seat gets the same full picture. Nothing is withheld from a seat to manufacture
difference between seats — an analyst short of context returns generic analysis, and generic
analysis occupies room in the synthesis without earning it.

Assemble one context block and send it to all of them:

- **The decision.** What is being chosen between, what would count as this having gone well,
  what is fixed and what is still open, what has already been settled and is not up for review.
- **The situation.** What exists today, what has already been tried, real capacity and
  constraints, relevant history. This is what makes a finding specific rather than generic, and
  it is the part most often left out.
- **The material.** Whatever the user has — documents, numbers, notes, prior work.

Two things to handle deliberately rather than by default:

- **Strip the advocacy.** If the user's material includes the case they have built *for* the
  idea — the pitch, the rationale, the reasons already marshalled — mark it as such rather than
  presenting it as neutral background. An analyst that reads a persuasive argument and then
  evaluates that argument tends to return the argument. Naomi is the exception: auditing the
  stated reasoning is precisely her job, so she gets it labelled and intact.
- **Do not send which way the user is leaning,** how much work they have already put in, or what
  they are hoping to hear. That is not context, it is pressure.

Write the block once, compactly. Eight seats each ingesting raw files is expensive; eight seats
reading one well-made brief is not.

## Step 2 — Choose the standing seats

The eight in `references/the-conclave.md` are the bench, not the lineup.

**Close to always right:**

- **Naomi** (assumptions) — almost every decision rests on something unverified.
- **Otto** (precedent) — almost every idea has been attempted in some form.
- **Cyrus** (the null option) — nobody who brings an idea has given not-acting a fair hearing.

**Seat when the question involves:**

| Seat | Seat it when |
|---|---|
| Cora | real resource commitment, or the user has limited capacity |
| Lena | anything with an intended audience other than the user |
| August | anything requiring cooperation the user does not control |
| Vera | the user is choosing scope, or the plan feels safe |
| Esther | the decision is expensive to reverse, or creates something ongoing |

**Leave a seat empty when it has nothing to work on.** A resource realist on a question with no
resource dimension writes filler, and filler in one sealed verdict makes every genuine finding
harder to pick out.

## Step 3 — Add the domain experts the question calls for

The standing seats supply reasoning stances, not subject knowledge. Some questions turn on a
fact only a specialist would know.

Add one to three based on what the question actually turns on. Ask: what would someone need to
know for their judgment to beat a smart generalist's? If the answer is nothing, do not add the
seat.

**Write a dynamic expert to the same depth as a standing seat.** Since the persona is the entire
source of differentiation, a thin one contributes a differently-worded copy of the analysis
everyone else already did. Give it all five fields from `references/the-conclave.md`:

- who they are, in two sentences of real professional background
- what they **attend to** first
- what they **discount** — the field that does the most work, and the one always skipped
- what **persuades** them
- what they **consider a failure**
- what they must return, concretely

**Label dynamic experts by function, not by invented name.** "The Compliance Read." "The
Technical Feasibility Read." Made-up names on domain experts imply a credential nobody holds, in
a way the standing bench does not — the bench is transparently a set of stances.

## Step 4 — Assign outside research

Research is an added capability, not a differentiator. It lets a seat reach facts the room does
not have.

- **Otto: mandatory.** Without it he is guessing about history.
- **Lena and August: yes** where an audience or a landscape can actually be researched.
- **Everyone else: when the question turns on a checkable external fact** their stance would
  care about. Naomi verifying a specific premise is a good use of it; Vera browsing is not.

More research is not automatically better. A panel where every seat searched converges on
whatever the top results say, which is its own kind of shared blind spot.

## Step 5 — Show the roster before running it

Present it as a table: seat, its one-line charge, whether it has research, and the model it will
run on. Then stop.

This is the user's steering moment and it is worth the interruption. They know which lens will
matter here and which will waste its context, and they will sometimes name a domain expert
nobody would have generated. Take the correction before spending the fan-out on the wrong lineup.

Five to nine analysts is a full panel. Past ten, the synthesis stops being a read on the question
and becomes a summary of a pile.

## Step 6 — Decide what the run should cost

A fan-out of this shape is expensive by construction: every seat is a full subagent doing real
analysis in its own context, and every seat now carries the full context block. That cost buys
the isolation and there is no version of this skill that avoids it. Two levers spend less of it.

**Seat fewer analysts.** The strongest lever, and free. Three well-chosen seats beat eight seated
out of a wish to be thorough, and an idle seat does not merely waste its context — it spends one
producing filler that then dilutes the synthesis. Step 2 is the cost control.

**Run the analysts on a faster model, and keep the synthesis on the strongest one available.**
Each seat holds a narrow, well-specified charge with a fixed output shape, which is work a
smaller model does well. The synthesis is different: it holds every verdict at once, weighs where
the lenses agreed, and sorts each conflict into factual or values. That is the reasoning worth
paying for.

The tradeoff is real and unevenly distributed. The seats that lose most on a smaller model are
the ones that range widest — **Otto**, whose research quality and willingness to report finding
nothing both degrade, and **Esther**, whose second-order chains shorten. **Naomi** and **Cyrus**
hold up well, because assumption audits and the case against acting are bounded tasks. When a
decision warrants it, put Otto and Esther back on the better model and leave the rest fast.

State the model split in the roster so the user sees what they are buying before it runs.

## KNOWN and INFERRED

Analysts reason from a mix of what the context block established and what they worked out. Tag
both, and carry the tags into the verdict:

- **KNOWN** — stated in the context, or found in something published and verifiable.
- **INFERRED** — reasoned from the question, the category, or the stance's own logic. Legitimate
  and unavoidable; much of it will be inferred. It just cannot be laundered into fact.

Written inline: `Available capacity: roughly six hours a week [INFERRED — not stated]`.

The synthesis reports which recommendations rest only on inferred ground and converts them into
questions to verify rather than findings to act on.

## Anti-patterns

- **The costume party.** Eight seats with distinct voices and identical substance. The single most
  common failure now that every seat shares the same information, and the fix is always in the
  persona: a lens that specifies only a personality changes the wording, while a lens that
  specifies what to discount and what counts as evidence changes the conclusion. If two verdicts
  differ only in tone, the two personas were not written far enough apart.
- **The zealot.** A seat that forces its stance past what the evidence supports, because it thinks
  its job is to represent a position rather than to judge from one. Guard against this by telling
  every seat plainly that reporting against its own disposition is the most valuable thing it can
  do.
- **The mouthpiece.** A seat that returns the user's own reasoning in costume. If a verdict echoes
  something already said in the conversation that produced the idea, either the advocacy material
  leaked in or the brief carried the user's framing. Re-run that seat with a cleaner brief.
- **The agreeable panel.** Eight seats that all like the idea. Occasionally real, usually a sign the
  brief carried the user's enthusiasm into the subagents. Check what was actually sent before
  believing it.
- **The generic seat.** A verdict that would survive find-and-replacing this question with a
  different one. Either the seat was briefed too thinly, or it was seated on a question its stance
  has nothing to say about.
- **The abstainer.** A seat that returns "this depends on factors not provided." Name what it
  depends on, state the assumption, and rule anyway. An analyst that will not commit has spent a
  full context to produce nothing.
