# Getting enough detail to analyze

The map is only as good as the description it is built on, and most people describe
their own workflow in about four sentences — because the parts they leave out are the
parts that have become automatic. Those omissions are where the interesting verdicts
live.

The goal is not a complete process document. It is enough detail to argue each step
from consequence.

## What the map needs

Per workflow:

- **Trigger and cadence.** What starts it, how often it runs, how many instances per
  week or term. Volume changes verdicts more than any other single fact.
- **Who does it**, and whether it is one person or a handoff chain.
- **What "done" means**, and who receives the output.
- **Time cost**, roughly — total, and which step eats the most.
- **Systems involved.** What has an export, what has an API, what is a PDF someone
  retypes, what requires logging into something with no integration.
- **Constraints.** Policy, regulatory, contractual, or accreditation requirements that
  govern how this must be done.

Per step, ideally:

- What the person is actually deciding or producing.
- What input they use, and where it comes from.
- What happens when this step is done wrong — and **who notices, and when**. This is
  the single most valuable answer in the intake and it is worth spending questions on.

## Scale the question budget

Assess what you were handed, then spend accordingly. Ask in one batch, numbered.

| What you have | Budget | Aim the questions at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A real SOP or process document | 1–3 | The steps the document skips because they are "obvious," plus failure and volume |
| A solid description, most steps named | 4–6 | Failure and detection per risky step, systems and exports, volume |
| A few sentences | 7–9 | Reconstruct the step sequence first, then trigger, volume, systems, failure |

Never exceed nine in the first batch, and never open a second round of the same size.
One follow-up batch of two or three is fine when the answers opened something real.
Past that, analyze with what you have and mark the unknowns.

## Question patterns that work

Ask for specifics and stories, not categories. The good version of each pair:

- Not "how long does this take?" → **"Walk me through the last time you did this. Where
  did the time actually go?"**
- Not "is this step important?" → **"If this step were skipped entirely, when would
  someone find out, and how?"**
- Not "could this be automated?" → **"What do you have to know to do this step that
  isn't written down anywhere?"**
- Not "what systems do you use?" → **"Where do you copy something from one place into
  another by hand?"**
- Not "what goes wrong?" → **"What's the mistake you're most careful not to make
  here?"**

The last two are the highest-yield questions in the set. Manual re-entry between
systems is nearly always an automate candidate, and the mistake someone is careful
about names the step where their attention is doing real work.

## Rules for the intake

- **One batch, numbered, then proceed.** People abandon a skill that interviews them.
  Ask everything at once so they can answer in a single sitting.
- **"I don't know" ends that thread.** Mark it unknown, note in the final map which
  verdicts are sensitive to it, and move on. Do not press.
- **Never ask what you can infer.** If the process is obvious from the document,
  reconstruct it and confirm rather than asking.
- **Do not ask for the material itself.** You need a *description* of the workflow, not
  the student records, applicant files, patient data, or client documents that flow
  through it. If the user offers such material, you do not need it to build the map,
  and say so.
- **Show your reconstruction before asking.** A numbered list of steps as you
  understand them, then the questions. Correcting a list is easier than answering an
  open question, and it usually surfaces two steps they forgot to mention.
