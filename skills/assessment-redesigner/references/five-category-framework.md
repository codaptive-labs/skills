# The Five-Category Framework for Assessment Redesign

The operational core of the redesign. Five directions an assessment can move once
AI can produce convincing work for the traditional version. They aren't a ranking —
they're five different answers to the same question: *how do we measure learning
when the output is no longer proof of it?* Most redesigns draw on one primary
category; the strongest often blend two.

Use this as a working reference: for each category, reach for it when the
description fits, then pick a concrete form and adapt it to the discipline.

---

## 1. AI-Resistant Design

**The idea.** When AI can generate sophisticated work indistinguishable from human
effort, integrity comes from requiring what can't be outsourced to an algorithm:
embodied presence, real-time judgment, interpersonal dynamics. The assessment moves
from a static product (the take-home essay, the problem set) to a visible, live
process — something that happens in real time, in a body, in front of witnesses.

**Reach for it when** the stakes are high, the course is synchronous or has contact
hours to spend, and you need assurance the student can actually do the thing — not
just submit a thing.

**Concrete forms.** Synchronous group problem-solving; improvisational challenge
sessions; oral defenses of submitted work; field-site or clinical reflection
debriefs; community-partnership work; process-documentation video; peer teaching;
live adaptation of a pre-built artifact (the student brings a draft — AI-assisted or
not — and must extend, defend, or repair it live).

**Watch for.** Accessibility and anxiety — live/oral formats must have equitable
alternatives. And don't confuse "hard to do with AI" with "measures learning";
keep the live task pointed at the real goal.

---

## 2. Exploratory AI Empowerment

**The idea.** Treating AI as contraband drives use underground and produces students
simultaneously dependent on and ignorant about these systems. Instead, make AI the
object of study: a microscope for examining how knowledge works. The student uses AI
openly and is assessed on the quality of that use and what they learned from it.

**Reach for it when** you want to build AI literacy directly, defuse the prohibition
dynamic, or the learning goal includes judgment *about* AI.

**Concrete forms.** AI sandbox challenges (get the tool to do something genuinely
hard, document how); prompt show-and-tell; a longitudinal Discovery Log tracking a
student's evolving relationship with the tools; an AI Failure Showcase that rewards
finding the tool's breaking points; assignments that make an AI-generated output the
*object of critique* rather than the illicit shortcut.

**Watch for.** Grade the thinking, not the tool's output. The assessment is the
student's evaluation, documentation, and judgment — not whatever the model produced.

---

## 3. Ethical AI Reflection

**The idea.** Constant availability of intellectual outsourcing creates ethical and
metacognitive questions earlier generations never faced. This category builds
explicit reflection into the work: recognizing that the student's own judgment is
precisely what can't be automated, and turning integrity from a compliance rule into
a personal commitment.

**Reach for it when** the course touches professional ethics, when you want students
to own their AI-use choices, or as a metacognitive layer on top of another
assessment.

**Concrete forms.** The "Why Me?" reflection (articulate the specific human judgment
you contributed that AI couldn't have); Cognitive Offloading Analysis (systematically
examine what you delegated to AI and at what cost to your own development);
Intellectual Provenance Inquiry (write a personal AI-use charter that governs your
behavior on a project). The habit underneath all of these: pausing to ask "am I
thinking right now, or just receiving?"

**Watch for.** Reflection can become performance. Ask for specifics and evidence,
not virtue signaling — the honest "I over-relied here and it cost me" is the win.

---

## 4. AI-Augmented Humanism

**The idea.** AI is a legitimate creative and intellectual collaborator; the point is
to make its contribution *documented* rather than hidden, and to assess the human
agency exercised alongside it. This is where most day-to-day collaborative use
actually lives, which is why it tends to be the most populated category in a
redesigned course.

**Reach for it when** the realistic future of the work involves AI collaboration,
and you want to assess how well the student directs, edits, and improves on it
rather than whether they used it.

**Concrete forms.** Co-creative studio projects with a documented human-vs-machine
contribution log; AI as a Socratic questioning engine rather than an answer engine;
Synthetic Student Instruction (teach a concept to an AI "novice" well enough that it
can apply it — which forces real clarity); AI-infused outlining with the student
owning structure and judgment; debate simulation; Handoff Protocol work (build with
AI, then stress-test each other's approaches as a group).

**Watch for.** Require the contribution to be visible. "I used AI throughout" is not
documentation; a marked-up trail of what the student decided, changed, and rejected
is.

---

## 5. Societal Co-Evolution

**The idea.** Individual AI mastery isn't enough when the technology is reshaping how
we work, create, govern, and relate. The widest-lens category asks students to
engage critically with what AI means for the world they're entering — positioning
them as people who will help determine that relationship, not just pass through it.

**Reach for it when** the course has room for a capstone or a big-picture unit, or
the discipline bears directly on policy, society, or the future of the field.

**Concrete forms.** AI Futures Council role-play negotiating binding policy; global
scenario planning across utopian / dystopian / pragmatic futures; civic-tech ethics
hackathons judged on social utility rather than speed; primary research on how
different populations actually use AI day to day; the most ambitious entry — "The
Ghost in the Shell" — evaluating the boundary between genuine AI capability and
sophisticated mimicry.

**Watch for.** Scope. These can balloon; anchor them to a concrete deliverable and a
discipline-specific question so they stay assessable.

---

## Choosing among them

- **Don't pick for the instructor — surface the fit.** Name which categories suit
  the learning goal and the course's constraints, with the trade-offs, and let them
  choose the redesign they're actually willing to run.
- **Blend deliberately.** A documented AI-collaboration project (4) with an oral
  defense (1) and a short offloading reflection (3) is often stronger than any single
  category alone.
- **Discipline-embed it.** The same category looks different in nursing, journalism,
  and economics. The test of a redesign is whether it reveals the judgment *this
  discipline* requires — evaluating an AI therapeutic recommendation, catching bias
  in AI-produced copy, spotting a graphical hallucination in an economic chart.
